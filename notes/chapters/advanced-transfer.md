This optional companion preserves the extended research case studies introduced in [finite viscous amplification](viscous.md). The main chapter develops one packet and one amplification argument. Here the question changes from one section to the next: how to compare derivatives, prepare a second wave, retain a useful region, extend its lifetime, or account for forcing.

## Shared notation and scale convention

The later constructions reuse the following conventions from the first finite-packet argument. Each source specifies its own amplitude and stopping time; a symbol reused for another purpose is identified where it appears.

Freeze one compact Gavrilov velocity profile $V$, its selected orbit, and its positive polarization exponent $\mu$. These do not vary with the concentration parameter $L$, which tends to infinity. Fix physical viscosity $\nu>0$. In rescaled coordinates, $h$ is the wave's oscillation scale, $\epsilon$ is viscosity and $\delta$ is the envelope width:

$$
h=L^{-1/10},\qquad \epsilon=\nu h^4,\qquad \delta=h^{1/2}.
$$

The proof works on the expanding torus with unnormalized norms and then rescales to the original torus at viscosity $\nu$. Write $q$ for the rescaled velocity. With velocity multiplier $A_L=L^{7/5}$, the map is $u_L(t,x)=A_Lq(A_LL t,Lx)$. Rescaled time is $\tau=A_LL t$, and a rescaled unnormalized $L^2$ norm acquires the factor $h/(2\pi)^{3/2}$ in the physical averaged norm. The initial datum changes with $L$; this is not one solution passing through infinitely many scales.

## Why self-cancellation is not error stability

Let $a$ denote local velocity amplitude, not normalized $L^2$ size. A packet of volume $\delta^3$ has $L^2$ size of order $a\delta^{3/2}$. For a tangent wave $W=aB\cos(S/h)$, where $B$ is a transverse polarization vector, the apparent $a^2/h$ self-advection cancels because $B\cdot\xi=0$. With its exact curl correction retained, the nonlinear residual has envelope scale $a^2/\delta$.

An arbitrary solenoidal error $e$ need not satisfy $e\cdot\xi=0$. Its interaction with the wave still includes a term of size $(a/h)(e\cdot\xi)B$. The [wave-handover note](../../references/navier-stokes/wave-handover.md) gives exact initial-rate counterexamples to replacing this generic stability cost by $a/\delta$. It also computes a nonzero normal slow velocity generated by pressure. That slow mean must be evolved with the full nonlocal pressure projection. These are exact identities and obstructions, not a theorem of sustained growth.

## To transfer strain, control derivatives of the error

A wave's velocity amplitude and its strain have different scales. For $a\sin(x/h)$, one derivative has amplitude $a/h$. Thus a small velocity disturbance can eventually dominate the host's strain even while its velocity remains small. Establishing this requires comparison in $C^1$, a norm controlling both the field and its first derivatives. An $L^2$ error bound alone does not suffice.

To obtain strain handover, increase the wave and shorten the observation window so that the accumulated error remains controllable. Correcting the phase average and the higher harmonics—integer multiples of the basic phase frequency—then lowers the residual enough to control derivatives. The decisive calculation is whether that improvement survives interpolation from the available error norms.

The [log-log handover theorem](../../references/navier-stokes/loglog-handover.md) keeps the generic error cost and changes the amplitude and time window:

$$
\ell=\sqrt{\log(1/h)},\qquad a_0=\frac h\ell,
\qquad T_h=\frac2\mu\log\ell.
$$

Here $h\to0$ is the rescaled oscillation scale, $\ell\to\infty$ is a slowly growing logarithmic parameter, $a_0$ is initial velocity amplitude, and $\mu>0$ is the frozen profile's polarization exponent. The central velocity amplitude grows from $h/\ell$ to order $h\ell$, while its strain grows from order $1/\ell$ to order $\ell$. The integrated gradient bound is only $C\ell$; consequently the energy propagation factor $e^{C\ell}=h^{-o(1)}$ loses less than every fixed negative power of $h$. Specifically, $o(1)$ denotes a quantity tending to zero as $h\to0$, and $C\ell/\log(1/h)=C/\ell\to0$. This sharper bound requires uniform control of the covector throughout the transported support, not merely a loose time-growth estimate.

The first approximation has propagated $L^2$ error $h^{9/4-o(1)}$. Small $L^2$ error alone does not guarantee small strain. The construction adds a full forced linearized-Euler mean and two tangent harmonic corrections, including the leading mean-to-wave phase interaction. Here “forced” describes an auxiliary correction equation; the final NS solution remains unforced. They give an improved full residual of order $h^{11/4}$ times fixed powers of $\ell$. All remaining harmonics and nonlocal mean tails are included in the reviewed residual accounting.

Why is that extra half-power decisive? With the corrected error and a conditional $H^s$ estimate, interpolation gives

$$
\|\nabla e\|_\infty
\le h^{1/4-5/(2s)-o(1)}.
$$

At the fixed index $s=12$, the exponent is $1/24-o(1)>0$, allowing a strict strong-solution bootstrap. Using the first approximation instead gives $-1/4-5/(4s)$, negative for every $s$. This calculation explains what the correction must accomplish; it does not replace verification of the residual estimate itself.

The final strain lower bound is evaluated at the **reference Euler** trajectory location with a chosen wave phase. It does not assert that the perturbed NS material particle follows that trajectory. After physical rescaling the data still change with $L$; this is not fixed-viscosity small-data $C^1$ instability. The physical final perturbation norm is of order $h^{11/4}\ell$. More strongly, the full solution satisfies an all-window high-pass upper bound $C_\nu[h^9\operatorname{poly}(\ell)+h^{11/4}\ell]=o(h^{11/10})$, so the earlier global activation target remains excluded throughout this interval.

In these formulas $\operatorname{poly}(\ell)$ denotes fixed powers of $\ell$ allowed by the proof, and $H^s$ controls derivatives through order $s$. The high-pass projection $P_{>H}$ retains modes with wavevector magnitude greater than $H$. All physical comparisons use the scale map in the shared notation section above.

## A finer linear amplifier on the actual outgoing wave

A large strain is not automatically another exponential amplifier. The outgoing wave resembles a shear, whose return map can stretch vectors despite having no eigenvalue greater than one. The elementary model is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Here $\gamma$ is the shear amount. For example, the unit vector $(0,1)$ is sent to $(\gamma,1)$, of length $\sqrt{1+\gamma^2}$. Both eigenvalues are one, while the largest singular value—the maximum length gain over all unit input vectors—is $(\sqrt{\gamma^2+4}+|\gamma|)/2$. After $m$ repetitions the upper-right entry is $m\gamma$, giving linear growth rather than an expanding Floquet multiplier. These exact shear identities describe the model mechanism; applying it to the actual first-stage NS solution requires a separate comparison.

The outgoing-wave theorem makes this comparison for a supplied finer seed in the exact linearized equation during a short late interval. That answers the amplification question for the chosen background. Preparing the seed and controlling its nonlinear feedback are separate steps.

The outgoing wave's leading gradient has the form $\kappa p\otimes N$, where $\kappa$ is its size, $p$ the velocity direction, $N$ the wave normal, and $p\cdot N=0$. Its square is zero because $(p\otimes N)^2=p(N\cdot p)\otimes N$. This is the nilpotent structure underlying the shear model above. In the estimates below, $h$ is the primary wave scale, $\ell=\sqrt{\log(1/h)}$, and $\epsilon=\nu h^4$ is rescaled viscosity.

The [outgoing-wave theorem](../../references/navier-stokes/outgoing-wave.md) supplies that comparison and a finite-wave realization in the **exact homogeneous viscous linearization** around the actual smooth first-stage solution $q$. Write $T_h$ for the first-stage terminal time. With

$$
t_0=T_h-\ell^{-3/4},\qquad k=h^{3/2},\qquad d=h^{5/4},
$$

it specifies a real, mean-zero, exactly solenoidal unit-$L^2$ seed at time $t_0$, localized to width $d$ and oscillating at wavelength $k$. Its initial radial high-pass norm above $1/k$ is $1-o(1)$. At $T_h$, the exact linearized solution has high-pass norm at least $c\ell^{1/4}$, after an all-mode error $h^{1/4-o(1)}$. This is a finite gain of order $(\log(1/h))^{1/8}$, not an exponential return multiplier.

Here the new phase follows the actual $q$-flow. The proof additionally tracks the original wave phase along an actual NS particle; it does not infer this phase locking from small gradient error alone. Fixed higher-order estimates control the first-stage cell derivatives. Nearby-carrier coherence costs $d/h=h^{1/4}$, the curl/envelope correction costs $k/d=h^{1/4}$, and principal viscous damping has scale $\epsilon/k^2=\nu h$. On the short terminal interval, propagation costs only $\exp(C\ell^{1/4})$, so the error still tends to zero. The pressure correction and the real packet's two Fourier lobes are both retained.

This earlier theorem treats a seed supplied at a late time in a linearized equation. The separate original-time theorem developed below adds a mixed receiver present from time zero and controls its nonlinear evolution. The physical rescaling preserves the gain ratio and the original fixed viscosity; the underlying first-stage data still vary with $L$.

An infinite construction must ultimately use one smooth initial datum and one viscosity, control every intervening error, and justify a finite terminal time. Neither finite handover nor the late-time linearized gain establishes that conclusion.

</details>

## Preparing the second wave at original time zero

Specifying a seed late in the evolution leaves its earlier history undetermined. The [original-time handover proof](../../references/navier-stokes/original-nonlinear-handover.md) instead supplies both packets in the initial datum. It constructs a smooth unforced NS solution whose second packet starts small in rescaled $C^1$, then produces strain larger than its first-stage host.

The useful change is to allow a mixed output polarization. Demanding a perfectly pure output can require a large initial seed to cancel an unwanted component. Allowing that component to remain makes the required preparation much cheaper. The proof then controls the entire flight and the nonlinear error, rather than only the endpoint matrix.

The finer frequency is already present at time zero. The conclusion concerns its preparation and amplification; it does not establish the production of a further seed. This is the distinction between a successful stage and a repeatable evolution developed in [iteration](iteration.md).

Keep $h\to0$ and $\ell=\sqrt{\log(1/h)}$ as above. The notation $A\asymp B$ means that their ratio stays between positive constants independent of $h$.

### A pure target can be expensive

Let $M$ be the full pressure-corrected polarization transfer between the initial and final transverse planes, expressed in the source's orthonormal bases. At the receiver, $p$ is the primary velocity direction, $n$ its normal direction, and the new covector $\xi$ points along $n\times p$. Write $t_f$ for the terminal time and $M^*$ for the adjoint (the transpose in real orthonormal coordinates). The exact determinant is

$$
r_h=\det M=\frac{|\xi(0)|}{|\xi(t_f)|}\asymp\ell.
$$

Requiring the final vector to be exactly $n$ fixes the initial vector to $M^{-1}n$. Its initial-to-final strain cost is

$$
C_{\mathrm{pure}}=r_h|M^{-1}n|=|M^*p|.
$$

The adjoint row measures how strongly initial polarizations reach the $p$ component. A large value makes *removing* that component expensive. If instead we require only $n\cdot Mb_0=1$, allowing a mixed final vector, Cauchy–Schwarz gives

$$
\min |b_0|=\frac1{|M^*n|},\qquad
C_{\mathrm{mixed}}=\frac{r_h}{|M^*n|}.
$$

The [matched transfer calculation](../../references/navier-stokes/original-nonlinear-handover.md) gives row sizes $|M^*p|\asymp\ell^3$ and $|M^*n|\asymp\ell^{5/2}$. Thus the pure cost grows like $\ell^3$, while the best mixed cost decreases like $\ell^{-3/2}$. The construction uses an explicit growing branch with these useful scales, without needing to realize the optimizing vector.

### Normalize the output, then count the strain

A bounded unit initial polarization $v(0)$ reaches components of orders $\ell^3$ along $p$ and $\ell^{5/2}$ along $n$. Set $k=h^{3/2}$. To obtain terminal normal strain $\ell^{7/8}$, multiply this branch by a velocity amplitude of order

$$
\frac{k\ell^{7/8}}{\ell^{5/2}}=k\ell^{-13/8}.
$$

The initial covector has size $\ell$, so the initial strain is
$(k\ell^{-13/8})(\ell/k)=\ell^{-5/8}$. At the receiver the covector has fixed size, and the larger $p$ component instead gives strain $\ell^{3-13/8}=\ell^{11/8}$. This exceeds the host’s $O(\ell)$ gradient. The normal component alone remains $\ell^{7/8}$; the conclusion concerns total added strain, observed at a specified particle of the base solution.

Write $Z$ for the prepared finer wave, including its localization. The same matching controls the entire history:

$$
\int_0^{t_f}|\xi(t)||v(t)|\,dt\le C\ell^3,
\qquad
\int_0^{t_f}\|\nabla Z(t)\|_\infty\,dt\le C\ell^{11/8}.
$$

This integral matters because arbitrary nonlinear errors pay a Gronwall factor depending on accumulated strain. Since $\ell^2=\log(1/h)$,
$\exp(C\ell^{11/8})=h^{-o(1)}$: it does not exhaust a positive power of $h$. Endpoint growth alone would not establish this budget.

The full mean and two harmonic corrections give residual $h^{31/8-o(1)}$, including pressure and nonlocal tails. A separate $H^{12}$ continuation argument upgrades the error to $C^1$ size $h^{1/48-o(1)}$. That is how the prepared wave becomes an actual smooth nonlinear solution rather than merely a polarization calculation.

### Supplied frequency is not generated frequency

The carrier’s central oscillation scale starts at $k/\ell$ and ends at order $k$. Both are finer than $h$; the finer component was present from time zero, and its endpoint wavelength becomes longer. This theorem establishes strain amplification, not automatic production of a finer frequency or a monotone spectral cascade.

Nor does the finite family assemble automatically into one smooth datum. The [seed-supply audit](../../references/analysis/smooth-seed-supply.md) differentiates the exact real curl at its central phase $\pi/2$. Its third derivative has size

$$
(k\ell^{-13/8})(\ell/k)^3=k^{-2}\ell^{11/8},
$$

with smaller envelope and curl errors. Infinitely many disjoint or quantitatively noncancelling copies on a compact common coordinate domain therefore cannot form a $C^3$ datum. This restricted obstruction leaves cancellation and generated or inherited seeds as separate questions. Physical rescaling must also be accounted for: the theorem’s small initial $C^1$ bound is in rescaled coordinates, and its physical gradient acquires the factor $A_LL$.

## A flat profile and a finite region of uniform strain

Preparing another wave requires information about a region, not just one particle. Replace the sinusoid with a smooth periodic profile that is linear near its center. This produces a finite ball where the actual solution's gradient is nearly constant at the endpoint. Viscosity rounds the linear portion; the heat estimate measures how much of the region retains the desired shape.

The spatial estimate will need a lifetime estimate before it can be used by another wave. It also gives a way to test the full solution for activity at a finer scale.

### Following the region after the endpoint

To follow the region, the
[later-flow proof](../../references/analysis/later-flow-attempt.md)
bounds the pressure across the whole domain and the diffusion entering
the region. It then transports the region along actual fluid particles
for a short additional interval of the same solution.

The solution continues another $c\ell^{-27/8}$ in rescaled time. For an
additional $\ell^{-5}$, a transported ball of radius comparable to
$k$ keeps its gradient within $O(\ell^{-1/4})$ of the endpoint matrix.
The velocity, viscosity and initial datum are the same as before. This
establishes short persistence of the existing strain. The estimate for a
new wave remains an upper bound; it does not prove that a new wave grows.

### Growing strain from a decreasing amplitude

For the illustrative local wave
$w(x)=a\sin(Kx_1)e_2$, the velocity size is $|a|$, while the
largest gradient has size $|a|K$. Halving the amplitude and tripling
the frequency multiplies the gradient by $3/2$. This calculation describes
one wave's shape; it does not assert that an arbitrarily chosen amplitude
and frequency history solves the fluid equation.

The [longer finite construction](../../references/navier-stokes/logarithmic-host.md)
establishes this separation of scales for the same supplied receiving wave in
an actual solution. Its focus is the earlier receiving time $t_f$.
After that time, its amplitude is bounded above and below
by fixed multiples of a decreasing function while its phase is compressed.
This does not mean the exact amplitude decreases at every instant.
Following the pressure and both polarization components is
essential: one coordinate changes sign, but the whole vector remains nonzero.
No new wave is installed to obtain this continuation.

### Improving an approximation can exclude a proposed next wave

Suppose an approximation resolves the existing waves but has too much error
to detect a much smaller one. Improving it can reveal a small signal; it
can also prove that the proposed signal is absent at the claimed size.
This is the second possibility in the
[finite-depth refinement](../../references/analysis/finite-depth-profile.md).

For every fixed $\eta>0$, frequency exponent $\beta>3/2$, and positive
power $P$, it gives, for sufficiently small $h$,

$$
\sup_{0\le t\le t_\eta}
\|\nabla P_{>h^{-\beta}}Q_h(t)\|_\infty\le h^P.
$$

Here $P_{>h^{-\beta}}$ is a Fourier projection; the unadorned $P$ in
$h^P$ is a chosen real exponent. The condition $\beta>3/2$ places the
cutoff strictly beyond the receiver's $h^{-3/2}$ power scale.
The proof chooses a finite derivative
order and a finite number of corrections **after** choosing the requested
power, and **before** taking $h$ small. Every correction starts at zero,
so this improves the description of the same solution without adding an
initial wave. A separate continuation estimate establishes its lifetime.

The phrase *smaller than every fixed power* has a precise limit: the
threshold for “sufficiently small” can depend on $P$. It does not say
the tail vanishes identically. It rules out a power-law-sized gradient in
the **full** finer-frequency tail during this interval; it does not bound
arbitrary pieces of a decomposition which might cancel one another. A coarser
component, a smaller seed, or growth at a later time needs its own analysis.
The result is in rescaled units and still concerns a family of data.

## The additional freedom and cost of forcing

The forced and unforced equations share the form

$$
\partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=\nu\Delta u+f.
$$

The unforced problem fixes $f=0$. A forced construction may choose
$f$, provided it satisfies the required smoothness and decay conditions
through the proposed singular time. This freedom is used in OpenAI's
published forced C/D result; the [published-proof chapter](published-proof.md)
explains its full construction and scope. The examples here isolate some
of the costs that must be met, within this project's finite-stage analysis.

For the periodic target, a force smooth through the terminal time can be
extended smoothly and cut off at a later time. This supplies the required
rapid time decay. The difficult estimate is uniform control of every mixed
space and time derivative as stages approach the terminal time. A small
integral of the force does not give those bounds; the
[force-admissibility calculation](../../references/navier-stokes/forced-admissibility.md)
states the simultaneous estimates a construction must meet.

For a worked example, take $u=a(t)\sin y\,e_1$, $p=0$. The nonlinear
advection vanishes, so the required force is
$(a'+\nu a)\sin y\,e_1$. The choice $a=(T-t)^{-1}$ gives a singular
force and fails the smooth-forcing requirement. If instead
$a'+\nu a=g(t)$ with smooth bounded $g$, the integrating-factor formula

$$
a(t)=e^{-\nu t}a(0)+\int_0^t e^{-\nu(t-s)}g(s)\,ds
$$

stays bounded on every finite interval. Prescribing a velocity and computing
its force is an exact calculation; admissibility of that force is a separate
part of a breakdown proof.

### Why a common diffusion rate can preserve a return

The [viscous return calculation](../../references/navier-stokes/forced-viscosity.md)
studies a wave mechanism in the separate
[Alpöge–Buckmaster smoothly forced Euler construction](https://cims.nyu.edu/~tristanb/euler.pdf). Its
principal wave model has two normalized amplitude coordinates, $T$ for
the circulation channel and $\Omega$ for the reduced-vorticity channel,
and selects a trajectory with $\Omega=0$ at the output. In the
corresponding axisymmetric NS equations, both coordinates have the same
leading diffusion rate. On one fixed supplied older flow, phase Fourier
mode $n\ne0$ therefore has amplitude vector $R_n=(T_n,\Omega_n)^T$
obeying the following principal system. Here $B(t)$ is the inviscid
two-component transfer matrix, $I$ is the identity matrix, $d(t)\ge0$
is the common fundamental-mode damping rate, and $D$ is its accumulated
clock:

$$
R_n'=(B(t)-n^2d(t)I)R_n,\qquad
R_n(t)=e^{-n^2D(t)}R_n^{\mathrm{Euler}}(t),\qquad
D(t)=\int_0^t d(s)\,ds,
$$

with matching initial amplitudes. To check the identity, differentiate
$e^{n^2D}R_n$: the diffusion term cancels, leaving the inviscid equation
$(R_n^{\mathrm{Euler}})'=BR_n^{\mathrm{Euler}}$. Multiplication by a
positive scalar preserves the direction of the pair. In particular, an
output with $\Omega=0$ still has $\Omega=0$ at the selected material
center, although its retained amplitude is smaller.

For the single mode $n=1$, the loss is $e^{-D}$. A general periodic
profile contains many modes and instead becomes
$e^{D\partial_s^2}F$; it is not one scalar multiple of $F$.
Differentiation commutes with this heat operator, so the same smoothing
also preserves the relation between the two channel profiles.

This calculation is exact for the stated amplitude ODE. A
[further change of variables](../../references/analysis/viscous-stage-attempt.md)
even makes the two full scalar diffusion operators identical. It still
does not make spatially varying transfer commute with heat.

To see the issue with ordinary differentiation, let $M(x)$ be a matrix
and $H(x)$ a vector. The product rule gives

$$
\partial_x^2(MH)=M\partial_x^2H+2(\partial_xM)(\partial_xH)
                  +(\partial_x^2M)H.
$$

Evolving $H$ by heat and then multiplying by $M$ misses the last
two terms. The stage analysis gives an exact example where those terms
create nonzero output in a channel that returned to zero without viscosity.
Thus the common operator is useful information, but its commutator with
the spatial transfer must also be controlled. This is a concrete use of
the composition defects studied earlier in the course.

### Check the geometry as well as the formulas

That Euler construction keeps its entire axisymmetric velocity
inside a fixed ring away from the symmetry axis. For ordinary NS with
smooth forcing, that whole-flow geometry has a
[regularity proof](../../references/analysis/fixed-annulus-obstruction.md):
bounded circulation and viscous dissipation give enough control to continue
the velocity smoothly. Improving a return inside that same fixed geometry
therefore cannot create the desired singularity.

This test concerns the full flow. It does not apply merely because one
small region looks axisymmetric. A construction approaching the axis or
breaking that symmetry needs its own transport, pressure, gain and force
estimates.

For a different finite example, the
[three-dimensional construction](../../references/analysis/threed-forced-stage.md)
uses a compact periodic host that equals
$Ax$, with $A=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,2\lambda,-3\lambda)$, in
its interior. A localized wave there has plateau amplitude and frequency

$$
a(t)=a(0)\exp\!\left(3\lambda t-
\frac{\nu K^2}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\right),
\qquad \kappa(t)=Ke^{-\lambda t}.
$$

At $\lambda=\nu K^2$ and $t=1/\lambda$, the velocity gain exceeds
12. Its frequency decreases, and the gradient gain has an extra factor
$e^{-1}$. The construction pays for the host, compact cutoffs, pressure,
activation and return. It is a smooth finite forced example. To repeat it
as a breakdown construction, the outgoing velocity would have to supply
the next stage under one compatible force budget; installing an arbitrarily
stronger host each time does not provide that argument.

The specified unchanged cutoff has a further obstruction: a circulation
calculation gives a necessary boundary-force cost that grows with the
wave's velocity amplitude on the fixed domain. Changing pressure cannot
remove it. An unbounded-output construction with bounded force therefore
needs a different velocity profile or additional velocity corrections.
Those changes must be checked in the full equation.

## Supplementary exercises

Exercises 1–4 remain in [the main chapter](viscous.md#exercises-test-the-conclusion-before-using-it). The following exercises retain their original numbers so that existing references to this material remain meaningful.

### 5. Distinguish common heat evolution from common amplitude loss

Let a periodic phase profile be $F(s)=\sin s+\sin(2s)$. After heat clock $D>0$, compute $e^{D\partial_s^2}F$. Can it equal $cF$ for one scalar $c$? Explain why the per-mode common damping of the two channels still preserves a selected principal zero in $\Omega$.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and feedback</summary>

The profile becomes $e^{-D}\sin s+e^{-4D}\sin(2s)$. The two factors differ, so no common scalar works when $D>0$. Within each fixed mode, however, both amplitude coordinates are multiplied by the same $e^{-n^2D}$; a zero reduced-vorticity output remains zero in that principal system at the selected center. This does not control the full stage's drift, feedback, localization or force.

</details>

### 6. Recover the interpolation threshold · advanced

With $\|e\|_2\le h^{11/4-o(1)}$ and $\|e\|_{H^{12}}\le h^{7/4-12-o(1)}$, use interpolation exponent $5/24$ to recover the gradient-error power.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and feedback</summary>

The exponent is $(19/24)(11/4)+(5/24)(7/4-12)=1/24$. Subpolynomial losses give $1/24-o(1)$, still positive for sufficiently small $h$. The calculation is conditional on both norm estimates and uniform interpolation constants. The positive power makes the gradient error tend to zero; the small $L^2$ error alone would not establish this.

</details>

### 7. Test terminal smoothness beyond the force's size

For $t<T$, set $g(t)=(T-t)\sin(1/(T-t))$, and consider the periodic force $f(t,x,y,z)=g(t)\sin y\,e_1$. This force is smooth for $t<T$ and tends uniformly to zero as $t\uparrow T$. Does it extend smoothly through the terminal time? Compute its first time derivative.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and feedback</summary>

With $s=T-t$,

$$
g'(t)=-\sin(1/s)+s^{-1}\cos(1/s).
$$

It is unbounded along sequences approaching $T$. The force has a continuous extension by zero at $T$, but no smooth extension there. Even a vanishing force amplitude does not supply the required control of every mixed space and time derivative. This is an admissibility check on a proposed force, not a construction of fluid breakdown.

</details>

### 8. Smaller than every power does not mean zero

Let $a(h)=e^{-1/h}$ for $0<h<1$. Show that for every fixed $P>0$,
$a(h)\le h^P$ for sufficiently small $h$, although $a(h)>0$.
Can one fixed positive $h$ make the inequality hold for every $P$?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and feedback</summary>

The inequality is equivalent to $1/h\ge P\log(1/h)$. With $x=1/h$,
this holds eventually because $\log x/x\to0$. Thus each fixed $P$
has a suitable threshold. For one fixed $h$, however, $h^P\to0$
as $P\to\infty$, while $e^{-1/h}>0$; no such common threshold works
for every $P$. The Fourier-tail result uses the first order of choices.

</details>
