# Generators and continuous change

A finite transformation records a destination without describing the
journey. The [previous chapter](transformations.md) followed
$T(x)=2x+1$. We now seek a continuous motion whose time-one map is
$T$. Two half-time motions must compose to the full update, and stopping
at any other intermediate time must give the same result. This consistency
will lead us from a family of maps to a differential equation.

Once the motion is known, the chain rule calculates the instantaneous rate
of any observable. That action is its **generator**. Integrating the rate
along the trajectory recovers the finite change. The order of these steps
matters: a rate at the initial state need not remain the rate throughout
the journey.

The analytic tools are the chain rule and the fundamental theorem of
calculus. We begin with autonomous flows, then allow measurements and
velocity fields to depend explicitly on time. The latter calculation is
the material derivative used in fluid mechanics.

If you have used only single-variable calculus, the
[bridge to partial derivatives](calculus-bridge.md#2-partial-derivatives-hold-the-other-coordinates-fixed)
works through the chain rule for a moving pair of coordinates before the
vector notation appears here.

## 1. A continuous family of transformations

**Definition 2.1 (flow).** Let $X$ be an open subset of $\mathbb R^n$. A continuously differentiable *flow* is a family of transformations $T_t:X\to X$, depending continuously differentiably on $(t,x)$, such that

$$
T_0=\operatorname{id}_X,\qquad T_{t+s}=T_t\circ T_s.
$$

For the initial discussion, assume these maps exist for all real $t$. A local flow satisfies the same identities whenever the states and times involved are within its domain of existence.

Examples include translation $T_t(x)=x+tc$, dilation $T_t(x)=e^t x$, and planar rotation through angle $t$. The law $T_{t+s}=T_t\circ T_s$ says that moving for time $s$ and then time $t$ has the same effect as moving for time $t+s$.

For our affine update, the tempting interpolation $A_t(x)=(1+t)x+t$ has the correct values at times zero and one. But $A_2(x)=3x+2$, whereas $A_1(A_1(x))=4x+3$. It fails the flow law. Instead, shift the coordinate to $z=x+1$, where the discrete update is multiplication by two. Multiplication by $2^t$ composes correctly because $2^{s+t}=2^s2^t$. Returning to $x$, we obtain

$$
T_t(x)=2^t(x+1)-1,\qquad X=\mathbb R.
$$

This gives the required update at $t=1$ and satisfies

$$
T_t(T_s(x))=2^t\bigl(2^s(x+1)\bigr)-1=T_{t+s}(x).
$$

Here $T_t$ is a map labelled by elapsed time $t$; its subscript is not an iterate. The flow law explains the relationship: for an integer $N$, the iterate $(T_1)^N$ equals the time-$N$ map $T_N$.

There is a restriction hidden in the request to fill in a finite update by a flow. Because

$$
T_t\circ T_{-t}=T_{-t}\circ T_t=T_0=\operatorname{id}_X,
$$

each map in a global flow has an inverse. Consequently $T(x)=x^2$ on $\mathbb R$ cannot be its time-one map: the update sends both $1$ and $-1$ to $1$, so it has no inverse on this state space. The finite calculus in the previous chapter still applies to it. Even when an interpolation by a flow exists, the time-one map need not determine it uniquely; Exercise 7 will give two different motions with the same full-time update.

Define the velocity field

$$
v(x)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}T_t(x)\right|_{t=0}.
$$

The flow law implies

$$
\frac{d}{dt}T_t(x)=v(T_t(x)).
$$

Indeed, $T_{t+h}(x)=T_h(T_t(x))$; differentiating with respect to $h$ at zero proves the formula. Thus a flow traces solutions of the autonomous differential equation $x'=v(x)$. Conversely, a continuously differentiable vector field has unique local solutions and a local flow. Global existence needs an additional argument and will not be assumed merely because $v$ is smooth.

For the affine motion just constructed,

$$
v(x)=(\log 2)(x+1),\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}T_t(x)=(\log2)\,2^t(x+1)=v(T_t(x)).
$$

The vector field gives the velocity at any possible state; a trajectory gives the successive states from one particular starting point. At the fixed state $x=-1$, the velocity is zero and the motion stays there.

Write $U_t=U_{T_t}$. The finite composition law gives $U_{t+s}=U_sU_t$. For a single autonomous flow, $T_t$ and $T_s$ commute, since $t+s=s+t$, so we also have $U_{t+s}=U_tU_s$. This does not imply that flows generated by two different vector fields commute.

## 2. Differentiating the observable action

**Definition 2.2 (generator on an observable).** At a state where the limit exists, define

$$
L_vf(x)
=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{\Delta_{T_t}f(x)}{t}
=\left.\frac{d}{dt}f(T_t(x))\right|_{t=0}.
$$

The limit here is taken at a fixed state. Later, when error is measured
over a whole region, convergence in a norm will be a separate question.

The types remain different: $v:X\to\mathbb R^n$ is a vector field, $f:X\to\mathbb R$ is a scalar observable, and $L_vf:X\to\mathbb R$ is another scalar function. We write $C^k(X)$ for functions with continuous derivatives through order $k$. In particular, the formula below defines $L_v:C^1(X)\to C^0(X)$ for continuous $v$; taking a second generator will need more differentiability.

**Proposition 2.3 (generator formula).** If $f$ is continuously differentiable, then

$$
\boxed{L_vf=Df\,v=v\cdot\nabla f.}
$$

Here $Df=(\partial_1f,\ldots,\partial_nf)$ is a row vector and $v$ is a column vector, so $Df\,v=\sum_i v_i\partial_i f$ is a scalar.

Moreover, along the flow,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(U_tf)(x)=(U_tL_vf)(x).
$$

**Proof.** Apply the chain rule to the scalar function $t\mapsto f(T_t(x))$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}f(T_t(x))
=Df(T_t(x))\,v(T_t(x)).
$$

At zero, $T_0(x)=x$, giving the generator formula. At arbitrary $t$, the right side is $(L_vf)(T_t(x))$, giving the second identity. $\square$

For translation with constant velocity $c$, the generator is the directional derivative $c\cdot\nabla$. For a spatially varying velocity, the direction depends on the state. In one dimension, $v(x)=x^2$ gives $L_vf=x^2f'$, on the times for which the flow exists.

For our affine motion and the square observable $f(x)=x^2$, the chain rule gives

$$
L_vf(x)=2(\log2)x(x+1).
$$

We can check it without operator notation:

$$
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[2^t(x+1)-1\bigr]^2\right|_{t=0}
=2x(\log2)(x+1).
$$

This is a rate at the initial state. At a later time $s$, the rate is $(L_vf)(T_s(x))$, which generally has a different value.

The operator is linear in $f$, even when its coefficient $v$ is nonlinear. As in the finite theory, this linearity does not by itself produce a closed finite system for a chosen collection of observables.

## 3. How the differential product rule emerges

**Proposition 2.4 (generator product law).** For continuously differentiable observables,

$$
L_v(fg)=fL_vg+gL_vf.
$$

**Proof from finite changes.** Divide the exact finite product law by $t$:

$$
\frac{\Delta_{T_t}(fg)}t
=f\frac{\Delta_{T_t}g}t
+g\frac{\Delta_{T_t}f}t
+\frac{(\Delta_{T_t}f)(\Delta_{T_t}g)}t.
$$

At each fixed state, differentiability gives $\Delta_{T_t}f=tL_vf+o(t)$ and the analogous expression for $g$; $o(t)$ denotes a remainder whose quotient by $t$ tends to zero. Each finite change is bounded in size by a constant times $|t|$ near zero. Their product divided by $t$ is therefore bounded in size by a constant times $|t|$, and tends to zero. Taking the limit proves the formula. $\square$

This proof identifies precisely what happens to the interaction term: it survives at finite steps and vanishes in this first-order limit. It can reappear in higher-order calculations. Repeated application of the generator product law gives

$$
L_v^2(fg)=gL_v^2f+2(L_vf)(L_vg)+fL_v^2g,
$$

whenever all the displayed derivatives exist.

The notation $L_v^2f$ means $L_v(L_vf)$, not $(L_vf)^2$. If $v$ varies with the state, the second application differentiates its coefficients as well as $f$. For example, in one dimension,

$$
L_v^2f=v(vf')'=vv'f'+v^2f''.
$$

The same chain-rule argument gives

$$
L_v(F\circ f)=F'(f)L_vf
$$

for a continuously differentiable scalar function $F$. This often helps us choose a more useful observable for a nonlinear evolution.

## 4. Recovering finite change by integration

**Proposition 2.5 (fundamental theorem along a flow).** Let $f\in C^1(X)$ and let $T_t$ be a continuously differentiable flow. Along any interval of existence,

$$
\boxed{f(T_t(x))-f(x)
=\int_0^t(L_vf)(T_s(x))\,ds.}
$$

Equivalently, $\Delta_{T_t}f=\int_0^t U_sL_vf\,ds$, interpreted pointwise.

**Proof.** Proposition 2.3 computes the derivative of $s\mapsto f(T_s(x))$. Integrate that derivative from zero to $t$ and use $T_0(x)=x$. $\square$

The integrand is evaluated along the moving state. Replacing it everywhere by its initial value generally changes the answer. If $L_vf$ remains constant along an orbit, the integral does reduce to $tL_vf(x)$; otherwise, its subsequent evolution matters.

For the affine motion, put $z=x+1$. Since $T_s(x)=2^sz-1$, the square's rate along the orbit is

$$
(L_vf)(T_s(x))=2(\log2)(2^{2s}z^2-2^sz).
$$

Using $\int_0^t a^s\,ds=(a^t-1)/\log a$ for $a>0$, $a\ne1$, integration gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_{T_t}f(x)
&=z^2(2^{2t}-1)-2z(2^t-1)\\
&=(2^tz-1)^2-(z-1)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

At $t=1$ this is $3x^2+4x+1$, exactly the finite difference in the previous chapter. At $x=0,t=3$, it gives $63-14=49$, matching our three discrete updates. Starting from zero, the initial rate is zero while the square increases by one over the first full update. Integration uses the rates at the states visited after time zero as well.

The same integral identity also measures the error of freezing the rate at
its initial value. Assume $v\in C^1$ and $f\in C^2$, so that
$L_vf$ is continuously differentiable. Apply the identity to $L_vf$
and integrate once more. For $t\ge0$, this gives

$$
f(T_t(x))
=f(x)+tL_vf(x)
+\int_0^t(t-s)(L_v^2f)(T_s(x))\,ds.
$$

To prove this, apply Proposition 2.5 to $L_vf$, substitute into the first integral, and reverse the order of integration over $0\le s\le r\le t$. In particular, if $|L_v^2f|\le M$ along this orbit segment,

$$
|\Delta_{T_t}f(x)-tL_vf(x)|\le\frac12 Mt^2.
$$

This statement needs a bound only along the segment in question. A formal infinite expansion such as $U_t=e^{tL_v}$ would require further interpretation and convergence assumptions; the finite integral remainder already gives a usable error estimate.

**Corollary 2.6 (continuous invariants).** A continuously differentiable observable is constant along every flow orbit if and only if $L_vf=0$ everywhere.

**Proof.** If the generator vanishes, Proposition 2.5 has a zero integrand. Conversely, differentiate the identity $f(T_t(x))=f(x)$ at zero. $\square$

## 5. Worked example: a nonlinear flow with a constant-rate observable

Consider

$$
x'=x(1-x),\qquad X=(0,1).
$$

The state remains between zero and one. Its flow is

$$
T_t(x)=\frac{e^t x}{1+(e^t-1)x}
=\frac{e^t x}{1-x+e^t x}.
$$

For every real $t$, the denominator is positive and greater than the numerator, so the formula remains in $X$. At $t=0$, it equals $x$. Differentiation verifies $\partial_tT_t=T_t(1-T_t)$.

We can also verify the flow law using the odds $r(x)=x/(1-x)$:

$$
r(T_t(x))=e^t r(x).
$$

Since $r$ is one-to-one on $(0,1)$, two successive transformations multiply the odds by $e^se^t=e^{s+t}$, proving $T_t\circ T_s=T_{t+s}$.

For the coordinate observable $q(x)=x$, the generator is

$$
L_vq=x(1-x).
$$

The exact finite change is

$$
\Delta_{T_t}q
=\frac{x(1-x)(e^t-1)}{1+(e^t-1)x}.
$$

It is not generally equal to $t x(1-x)$, since the growth rate changes as the state moves.

How can we find an observable with a simpler rate? To ask for $L_vf=1$ is to ask for $x(1-x)f'(x)=1$. Solving for $f'$ and using partial fractions gives

$$
f'(x)=\frac1{x(1-x)}=\frac1x+\frac1{1-x}.
$$

Integrating gives $\log x-\log(1-x)$, up to a constant. Thus we choose the logarithm of the odds,

$$
f(x)=\log\frac{x}{1-x}.
$$

Then

$$
f'(x)=\frac1{x(1-x)},\qquad L_vf=1.
$$

Proposition 2.5 gives the exact identity

$$
f(T_t(x))-f(x)=t.
$$

For instance, starting at $x=1/4$, the initial odds are $1/3$. At time $t=\log3$, they equal one, so $x$ reaches $1/2$. The logarithmic observable increases by $\log3$ throughout this motion, although the coordinate increases by only $1/4$.

The transformed observable exposes a simpler evolution law. Its domain matters: it is not defined at the endpoint equilibria $0$ and $1$. Those states belong to an extended logistic system, but this coordinate does not describe them.

The coordinate observable also illustrates the error estimate. Its second generator is

$$
L_v^2q=x(1-x)(1-2x).
$$

On $(0,1)$, its absolute value is at most $1/4$. Therefore for $t\ge0$,

$$
|T_t(x)-x-tx(1-x)|\le\frac18t^2.
$$

For example, a time step $t=0.1$ makes the first-order prediction
accurate to within $0.00125$ for every initial state in $(0,1)$.
Repeated approximate steps also propagate errors inherited from earlier
steps; [Composition and error](composition.md) will account for that
additional effect.

## 6. Time-dependent observables and the material derivative

A moving particle may encounter a field that also changes with time. Assume $v(t,x)$ is continuously differentiable on an open region of time and space, and restrict attention to times for which its solutions exist. Let a particle's path solve

$$
\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=v(t,x(t)),
$$

and let $f(t,x)$ be a continuously differentiable scalar field. Write $\Phi_{t,s}(x)$ for the position at time $t$ of the particle starting from $x$ at time $s$. This evolution uses two times because a time-dependent velocity may produce different motions over intervals of equal length.

Its composition rule keeps the intermediate time visible:

$$
\Phi_{t,r}\circ\Phi_{r,s}=\Phi_{t,s},\qquad
\Phi_{s,s}=\operatorname{id},
$$

whenever the trajectories involved exist. The output at time $r$ becomes the input to the next map starting at time $r$.

**Definition 2.7 (material derivative).** Define

$$
D_vf=\partial_tf+v\cdot\nabla_xf.
$$

**Proposition 2.8 (accumulation along a time-dependent motion).**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&f(t,\Phi_{t,s}(x))-f(s,x)\\
&\quad=\int_s^t(D_vf)(\tau,\Phi_{\tau,s}(x))\,d\tau.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Proof.** The chain rule along $\tau\mapsto(\tau,\Phi_{\tau,s}(x))$ gives

$$
\frac{d}{d\tau}f(\tau,\Phi_{\tau,s}(x))
=\partial_\tau f+\nabla_xf\cdot v,
$$

with both terms evaluated at the moving point. Integrating proves the result. $\square$

The partial derivative $\partial_tf$ measures change at a fixed location. The material derivative measures change along a particle. A field may vary at fixed locations while every particle carries a constant value.

We can recover this rule from the generator definition by enlarging the state space to include time. The extended velocity is $\widetilde v(t,x)=(1,v(t,x))$, and its local flow is

$$
\widetilde T_\tau(t,x)
=(t+\tau,\Phi_{t+\tau,t}(x)).
$$

Acting on $f(t,x)$, its generator is exactly $D_v$. Including time in the state turns the two-time evolution into an autonomous flow on the larger space.

## 7. Worked example: a field carried by a shear

Return to the planar shear from the finite chapter, now letting its amount
vary continuously with time. A particle's position changes, but a number
carried by that particle may remain constant. Predict how a circular
pattern of such numbers will look after the shear, then compare the two
ways of measuring change below.

Let $v(x,y)=(y,0)$. A particle starting at $(x_0,y_0)$ follows

$$
T_t(x_0,y_0)=(x_0+ty_0,y_0).
$$

Suppose each particle carries a number whose initial spatial distribution is $f_0(x,y)=x^2+y^2$. What field describes those numbers later? The particle now at $(x,y)$ started at $(x-ty,y)$. Looking up its initial value gives

$$
f(t,x,y)=(x-ty)^2+y^2.
$$

Equivalently, $f(t,\cdot)=f_0\circ T_{-t}$. The inverse flow appears because we start at a current location and trace back to the particle's initial location. By contrast, $U_tf_0=f_0\circ T_t$ evaluates the fixed observable $f_0$ at the future position of a particle. These two functions answer different measurement questions.

At a fixed point,

$$
\partial_tf=-2y(x-ty),
$$

which is generally nonzero. But the transport contribution is

$$
v\cdot\nabla f=y\partial_xf=2y(x-ty).
$$

Hence $D_vf=0$. Direct substitution confirms the material calculation:

$$
f(t,x_0+ty_0,y_0)=x_0^2+y_0^2.
$$

The shearing motion changes the shape of the field while preserving its value on each moving particle.

Compare the fixed observable $g(x,y)=x^2+y^2$. Its generator is $L_vg=2xy$, so the accumulated change is

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(T_t(x,y))-g(x,y)
&=\int_0^t2(x+sy)y\,ds\\
&=2txy+t^2y^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is the initial rate multiplied by time. The second comes from the rate changing along the orbit. Here $L_v^2g=2y^2$ and $L_v^3g=0$, so the second-order expansion is exact. At $(x,y)=(0,1)$, the initial rate is zero but the finite increase is $t^2$: zero initial derivative does not imply invariance.

## 8. Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Translation.** Let $T_t(x)=x+ct$ and $f(x)=e^{ax}$, for real constants $a,c$. Find $L_vf$ and $\Delta_{T_t}f$, and verify Proposition 2.5 directly, including the case $ac=0$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

The velocity is $v=c$, so $L_vf=ac e^{ax}$, whereas $\Delta_{T_t}f=e^{ax}(e^{act}-1)$. If $ac\ne0$,

$$
\int_0^t (L_vf)(T_s(x))\,ds
=ac e^{ax}\int_0^t e^{acs}\,ds
=e^{ax}(e^{act}-1).
$$

If $ac=0$, both the generator along the orbit and the finite change are zero, so the integral identity holds without division by $ac$.

</details>

**Exercise 2 — Rotation.** For $v(x,y)=(-y,x)$, calculate $L_v(x^2+y^2)$. Use Corollary 2.6 to explain the result. Then calculate $L_vx$ and $L_v^2x$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

The generator is $-y\partial_x+x\partial_y$. Acting on $x^2+y^2$ gives $-2xy+2xy=0$, so squared distance from the origin is constant along the flow. Also $L_vx=-y$ and $L_v^2x=L_v(-y)=-x$, consistent with rotation. In particular, $L_v^2x$ is not the square of $L_vx$.

</details>

**Exercise 3 — Where the generator exists.** Let $T_t(x)=x+t$ and $f(x)=|x|$. Compute the finite difference at $x=0$. Does the two-sided generator limit exist there? Determine the generator at every nonzero state.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

At zero the finite difference is $|t|$. The quotient $|t|/t$ has different limits from the two sides, so the generator does not exist there in the stated sense. At nonzero states it equals $\operatorname{sgn}(x)$: $1$ for $x>0$, and $-1$ for $x<0$. Finite transformation calculus remains meaningful even at a point where the infinitesimal limit fails.

</details>

**Exercise 4 — An accelerating translation.** For $v(t,x)=t$, find $\Phi_{t,s}(x)$. Show that $f(t,x)=(x-t^2/2)^2$ has zero material derivative, and verify its constancy along trajectories.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution</summary>

Integrating the velocity from the starting time $s$ to the final time $t$ gives
$$
\Phi_{t,s}(x)=x+\frac{t^2-s^2}{2}.
$$
Writing $z=x-t^2/2$, we have $\partial_tf=-2tz$ and $v\partial_xf=2tz$, so $D_vf=0$. Substitution gives
$$
f(t,\Phi_{t,s}(x))=(x-s^2/2)^2=f(s,x).
$$

</details>

**Exercise 5 — A smooth velocity with a finite lifetime.** Solve $x'=x^2$ starting at $x_0>0$, and determine the largest time interval containing zero on which the solution exists. The vector field is smooth on $\mathbb R$; why does it fail to give a flow on all of $\mathbb R$ for all real times?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint and solution</summary>

Along a nonzero solution, differentiate the reciprocal: $(1/x)'=-1$. Thus

$$
\frac1{x(t)}=\frac1{x_0}-t,\qquad
x(t)=\frac{x_0}{1-tx_0}.
$$

For $x_0>0$, its maximal interval through zero is $( -\infty,1/x_0)$. The solution tends to infinity at the finite right endpoint. The same rational formula on $t>1/x_0$ does not extend this trajectory through the missing time. Smoothness gives local existence and uniqueness; it does not prevent the state from leaving every bounded set in finite time.

</details>

**Exercise 6 — A step versus a flow.** The exact flow of $x'=-x$ is $T_h(x)=e^{-h}x$. The Euler update is $E_h(x)=(1-h)x$. Define the observable discrepancy by $\delta_hf=U_{E_h}f-U_{T_h}f$. Compute it on $q(x)=x$ and on $q^2$. For $0<h<1$, compare $E_h^N(x)$ with $T_{Nh}(x)$. When do these states agree for a fixed positive integer $N$?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and the next question</summary>

Direct substitution gives

$$
\delta_hq=(1-h-e^{-h})x,\qquad
\delta_h(q^2)=\bigl((1-h)^2-e^{-2h}\bigr)x^2.
$$

Repeated updates give $E_h^N(x)=(1-h)^Nx$, while $T_{Nh}(x)=e^{-Nh}x$. For $0<h<1$, the inequality $\log(1-h)<-h$ gives $0<(1-h)^N<e^{-Nh}$. Hence the two states agree only for $x=0$. To prove the logarithmic inequality, integrate $1/(1-s)>1$ from zero to $h$.

Both methods have the same first-order rate as $h\to0$, but different finite updates. For a fixed total time $t$, setting $h=t/N$ does give $(1-t/N)^N\to e^{-t}$. The [temporal error chapter](composition.md) quantifies such accumulated error when an exact formula is unavailable.

</details>

**Exercise 7 — The same update, different generators.** On $\mathbb R^2$, compare the stationary motion $A_t(x,y)=(x,y)$ with the rotating motion

$$
B_t(x,y)=\bigl(x\cos(2\pi t)-y\sin(2\pi t),\;
                 x\sin(2\pi t)+y\cos(2\pi t)\bigr).
$$

Verify that both are flows and that $A_1=B_1$. Find their velocity fields and generators on $q(x,y)=x$. Starting at $(1,0)$, compare the values of $q$ at time $1/2$. Does knowing the state after every integer time determine the motion between those observations?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution: finite observations do not fix the intervening motion</summary>

The stationary maps satisfy the flow law immediately. For $B_t$, the sine and cosine addition formulas give $B_t\circ B_s=B_{t+s}$, and $B_0$ is the identity. One full turn brings every point back, so both time-one maps are the identity.

Differentiating the maps at $t=0$ gives

$$
v_A=(0,0),\qquad v_B=2\pi(-y,x),
\qquad L_{v_A}q=0,\qquad L_{v_B}q=-2\pi y.
$$

From $(1,0)$, the stationary motion has $q=1$ at time $1/2$; the rotation has $q=-1$. They agree again at every integer time. The first derivative of $q$ is even zero initially for both of these particular trajectories, but the rotation has $L_{v_B}^2q=-4\pi^2x$. A generator describes the continuous motion and supplies more information than one finite update. The update alone does not select a unique generator.

</details>

## Further reading

For scalar observables, $L_v$ is the classical *Lie derivative* along a vector field. Its definition by differentiating pullbacks, and its derivation property, are standard in differential geometry; see Appendix B of Evans and Zworski's [*Lectures on Semiclassical Analysis*](https://math.mit.edu/classes/18.157/S2012/semiclassicalEvansZworski.pdf). The operator viewpoint also connects to the Koopman lineage described in the [finite transformations chapter](transformations.md). The material derivative is the corresponding chain rule for quantities observed along moving particles.

For a systematic development of existence, uniqueness, dependence on
initial data and flows, see Gerald Teschl,
[*Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems*](https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-ode/)
(American Mathematical Society, 2012), available from the author's
University of Vienna page. Its existence and continuation arguments supply
the theory behind the domains of the flows used here. A useful reading
exercise is to revisit $x'=x^2$ and identify which hypothesis permits
continuation and which one fails at its finite endpoint.
